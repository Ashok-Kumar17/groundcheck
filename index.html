What this does

  1. A small language model running on your GPU splits the text into checkable claims.
  2. Only short names and keywords go to Edgepedia to fetch the relevant paragraphs. Your text stays on your device.
  3. The model must quote the exact source sentence. Plain code then compares the details, so the model can't just say "looks fine".

Each true claim is stated in its Edgepedia article. Each false one is the same fact with one wrong year, number, person, place or order, the kind of mistake chatbots make. The number that matters most is false claims shown as green.

Why it's built this way

Small models agree with everything

Asked "is this claim supported?", a 1.5B model says yes to nearly any confident sentence, including false ones. So the model isn't asked to judge. It quotes a sentence, writes down what the claim says and what the source says, and plain code compares the two.

No quote, no verdict

Every verdict must point at a sentence that actually exists in the retrieved text. If the "quote" isn't there, the model invented its evidence, and the claim drops to couldn't verify. A wrong green tick is worse than an honest "don't know".

The details must match

A "supported" verdict stands only if every number, date, ordinal ("third") and proper name in the claim also appears in the evidence. This catches the most common AI error: the right fact with the wrong year.

Private by design

The document never leaves the device. Edgepedia only receives the names to look up (for example "Chandrayaan-3") and a few keywords per claim. The trace tab counts every byte sent.
